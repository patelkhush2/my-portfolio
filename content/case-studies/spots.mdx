## 00 / Introduction

### From Inspiration To Itinerary.

Product Design · Web Application<br />
Role: Sole Product Designer<br />
Scope: Product Architecture, UX/UI, Interaction Design, Visual System<br />
Team: CEO, Product Managers & Engineers<br />
Status: In progress, not yet shipped

Spots turns the places people save into trips they can actually take. I'm the sole product designer on a small NYC startup team, working with the CEO, product managers and engineers across discovery, planning and itineraries. The product hasn't shipped yet. This case study covers the decisions behind the current design and the reasoning for each one.

## 01 / The Opportunity

### Finding Places Is Easy. Making Plans Is Harder.

People save places all the time, from friends, creators and their own browsing. But when it's time to plan a trip, those saves sit in one app while the actual planning happens across notes, spreadsheets and browser tabs. Saving feels like progress, but nothing carries forward.

Two things make this harder than building a better list. Trips start in different places: a destination, a budget, a friend's recommendations, or just a few free days. And a plan is never right the first time. Spots started from one question: what if a saved list could be the first draft of a trip? Every decision below comes back to it.

## 02 / Defining the Product

### Three Activities. One Thread.

I structured Spots around three activities, because each one asks a different question. Discover asks where is worth going. Plan asks what this trip should look like. Organize asks what happens on each day.

The harder part was deciding what carries between them. A saved place has to arrive in planning with its context, like who recommended it and what kind of place it is. A plan has to arrive in the schedule as something you can edit, not a block of text.

## 03 / Designing the Experience

### Decision 01 — Saving Shouldn't Be A Dead End.

A list was something you browsed, with no path into a plan. So I made every list a starting point. Open any list and planning is one step away, already using those places as context. The trade-off is that lists feel less like finished collections and more like raw material. That's intentional: a saved list becomes the first draft of a trip.

### Decision 02 — Start From Whatever You Have.

Search fields assume you already know the destination and dates. A blank chat assumes you know what to ask. Neither fits a trip that starts with a screenshot and a friend's list. So I combined them. A short trip bar holds the facts: where, how many days, how many travelers. The chat input accepts whatever you already have, whether that's an image, a saved list or a PDF of notes. Suggested prompts show what's possible before you type. The result is that you can start planning without organizing anything first.

### Decision 03 — Refine The Plan, Don't Rebuild It.

A long chat reply is hard to compare and harder to change, and re-prompting to swap one hotel throws away your progress. So answers become interface. You compare a few trip options at a glance, then open one as an itinerary you can review day by day. Each part of the plan, like where you're staying, shows its cost next to quick refinements, so you can change one piece without starting over. Conversation creates the plan, and small, specific changes shape it.

## 04 / Designing the System

### One Way To Plan, Everywhere.

I explored many home, discovery and navigation layouts, shown below. The direction that held up was the one with the fewest entry points. Top-level navigation stays at two items, and the same planning input appears wherever a trip can begin, whether that's on a list, on a place or in chat. There's one way to plan, not three. Detail views, like a list or a stay, open in the same side panel, so a new screen still feels familiar.

## 05 / The Product

### Discover. Plan. Go.

Start from places you've saved. Add the basics: where, how long, who's coming and the budget. Compare a few options, pick one, adjust the parts that don't fit, and review it day by day. One path from a saved place to a trip you can take, without leaving Spots.

## 06 / Reflection

### Design The Connections, Not Just The Screens.

As the only designer, I found that most of the work wasn't on individual screens. It was deciding what carries from one screen to the next: which context a saved place brings into planning, which controls stay the same, and when the interface should change shape.

Spots hasn't shipped and hasn't been tested with users yet, so I can't claim outcomes. The first two things I'd test are whether people understand that a saved list can become a trip, and whether one-tap refinements feel faster than re-prompting. Those answers would tell me whether the core idea holds: making the distance between finding a place and going there smaller.
